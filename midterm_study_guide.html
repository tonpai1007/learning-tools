Chapter 1: Introduction to Machine Design

What is Mechanical Design?

  • Mechanical design = the process of designing elements of a machine so they can perform a task efficiently, safely, and reliably.
  • Key design factors: functionality, strength, safety, cost, manufacturability, reliability, maintainability.

Design Factor vs. Safety Factor

TermDefinitionSymbol
Design Factor (n_d) The planned or target factor chosen by the designer nd
Safety Factor (n) The actual factor based on the real loads and material:
n = S_allowable / σ_actual		 n
⭐ KEY EXAM CONCEPT: Safety factor should always be greater than or equal to the design factor. A good design has n ≥ nd. The safety factor in the past exam answer was "at least 3 times" the design factor.
Design Factor:
nd = Sallowable / Srequired
S_allowable = maximum usable strength (yield or ultimate depending on failure mode)

📝 Exam Question Type (from answer key)

"Which is better: design factor or safety factor?" — Safety factor is better because it's based on the actual applied load (must be ≥ 3× design factor).

"Force = 5000 N, area = 10 mm², factor = ?" — Answer: Force/Area = Safety factor = 5000/10 = 50

Load Types

  • Static load — applied slowly, remains constant
  • Dynamic/Impact load — applied suddenly, changes with time
  • Cyclic/Fatigue load — repeated fluctuating load

2.2 Stress & Strain

Stress: σ = F / A₀  [Pa or MPa]
Strain: ε = (l − l₀) / l₀  [unitless]
Young's Modulus: E = σ / ε = slope of elastic region
⚠️ Engineering vs. True Stress:
  • Engineering stress: uses original cross-sectional area A₀ (assumed constant)
  • True stress: uses the instantaneous (actual) area at that point
  • Since the area shrinks during tension → true stress > engineering stress
  • True strain (logarithmic): ε = ln(l/l₀) = ∫dl/l

Stress-Strain Diagram — Key Points

Point/RegionMeaning
Proportional limitWhere σ ∝ ε; slope = Young's modulus E
Elastic limitSeparates elastic and plastic deformation
Yield pointε increases rapidly but σ does NOT increase
Ultimate strengthMaximum stress on the diagram
Fracture pointMaterial breaks
Offset method (0.2%)When yield point is hard to find, draw line parallel to elastic region from ε = 0.002
⭐ KEY EXAM CONCEPT (from answer key): Engineering stress assumes the area stays constant; true stress uses the actual area at that point. Also: malleability = deformation under compression; ductility = deformation under tension.

2.3 Hardness

MethodHow it worksFormula
Rockwell Indenter with minor force F₀, then major force F₁. Hardness = difference in penetration depths HR = E − e
Brinell Indenting ball with standard force, measure indentation diameter HB = 2F / [πD(D − √(D²−d²))]

Brinell ↔ Ultimate Strength Relations

For steel:
Su = 0.5·HB [kpsi]   or   Su = 3.4·HB [MPa]

For cast iron:
Su = 0.23·HB − 12.5 [kpsi]   or   Su = 1.58·HB − 86 [MPa]
⭐ EXAMPLE (from Chapter 2):
1040 hot-rolled steel, target Su = 100 kpsi → HB = Su/0.5 = 200.
Given HB = 275 for steel → Su = 3.4 × 275 = 935 MPa.
Grey cast iron HB = 200 → Su = 0.23(200) − 12.5 = 33.5 kpsi (ASTM No. 35).

2.4 Impact Strength

  • Impact load = load applied in time < 1/3 of the lowest natural period of vibration
  • Measured by Charpy test (specimen horizontal, notch struck from back) or Izod test (specimen vertical, struck at upper tip)
  • Critical temperature: temperature where material transitions from ductile → brittle (brittle-ductile transition)
  • Strain rate ↑ → strength ↑ (yield increases faster than ultimate)

2.5 Metal Forming

Hot WorkingCold Working
Definition Forming above re-crystalline temperature Forming at low temp (typically room temp)
Processes Rolling, extrusion, forging Rolling, drawing, extrusion, turning, grinding, polishing
Grain size Changed (refined) → strength↑, ductility↑ Not changed but distorted

Ductility ↑ ↓ (strain hardening)
💡 Strain Hardening: When a cold-worked metal is loaded beyond yield, unloaded, then reloaded — the new elastic line is nearly parallel to the original but shifted. Yield strength increases, ductility decreases. Repeated cycles → more brittle → eventually breaks.

2.6 Heat Treatment of Steel

ProcessMethodEffects
Annealing Heat above critical temp, cool slowly in furnace Ductility ↑, strength ↑, residual stress released
Normalising Heat above critical temp, cool in atmosphere Ductility ↑, strength ↑, finer grain than annealing
Quenching Heat above critical temp, cool rapidly in water/oil/air Hardness ↑, brittleness ↑, wear & vibration resistance ↑
Tempering Reheat below critical temp after quenching Hardness ↓, brittleness ↓ (residual stress released)
Case Hardening Increase carbon at surface, then quench & temper Outer surface hardness ↑, core ductility & toughness unchanged

2.9 Non-Ferrous Metals

MetalAdvantagesDisadvantages
Aluminium Great strength-to-weight, corrosion resistant (oxide coating), high thermal & electrical conductivity, low melting point Lower tensile strength than steel, 1/3 stiffness of steel, difficult to solder/braze/weld

Titanium As strong as steel but ½ weight; corrosion resistant; nonmagnetic; high-temp strength Expensive, difficult machinability, low thermal conductivity
Copper Usually as alloys: Brass (Cu + Zn), Bronze (Cu + Si/P/Al/Be) —

Brass types (Cu + Zn)

  • Low zinc (5–15%): easy cold work, ductile, corrosion resistant, hard to machine
    • Gilding brass (5% Zn) — jewelry
    • Commercial bronze (10% Zn)
    • Red brass (15% Zn) — tubing, radiators
  • Medium zinc (20–36%): cheaper, better machinability
    • Cartridge brass (30% Zn) — best ductility+strength combo
    • Yellow brass (35% Zn) — many fabrication options
    • Admiralty metal (28% Zn + 1% Sn) — saltwater corrosion resistance
  • High zinc (36–40%): less ductile, hot work only
    • Muntz metal (40% Zn) — cheap
    • Naval brass (40% Zn + 0.75% Sn) — better corrosion resistance

Bronze types (Cu + other)

  • Phosphor bronze: fatigue/corrosion/wear resistant, good for springs
  • Beryllium bronze: very corrosion resistant, high strength, expensive
  • Aluminium bronze: better strength & corrosion than brass
  • Silicon bronze: similar properties to mild steel, easy to work

2.10 Ceramics

  • Inorganic compounds of metal + non-metal; covalent + ionic bonds (stronger than metallic)
  • → hardness ↑, stiffness ↑, ductility ↓, melting point ↑
  • No free electrons → not electrically/thermally conductive
  • 3 categories:
    • Traditional: natural silicates (clay), silica (SiO₂), alumina (Al₂O₃), SiC
    • New: oxide ceramics, carbides (SiC, WC, TiC), nitrides (Si₃N₄, BN, TiN)
    • Glasses: silica-based (50–75% SiO₂); additives: MgO, CaO, Na₂O, PbO, B₂O₃

2.11 Polymers

TypeDefinitionExamples
Thermoplastic Can be melted & remolded when heated; recyclable (no molecular change) PE, PP, PC, PET, PS, PVC, ABS, TEFLON, nylon, acrylic, polyurethane
Thermoset Cannot be remolded after polymerisation; strong molecular bonds; burns if reheated Phenolic, urea, melamine, epoxy, polyester
⚠️ All plastics are polymers, but NOT all polymers are plastics. (Elastomers/rubbers and natural polymers like DNA/cellulose are polymers but not plastics.)

2.12 Composite Materials

  • Formed from two+ dissimilar materials: Filler (discontinuous reinforcement, stiff & strong) + Matrix (holds filler, transfers load, usually plastic resin)
  • Properties vary by direction → need multiple plies
  • Up to 5× strength-to-weight of steel, 8× stiffness-to-weight of metals
  • Used in automotive, marine, aircraft, spacecraft

2.13 Material Selection Process

3 Steps:
1. List & prioritise all required properties
2. For each property, list & prioritise all available materials
3. Select material which appears in all property lists

Chapter 3: Load Analysis

3.1 Equilibrium

2-D Equilibrium (3 equations):
ΣFx = 0   ΣFy = 0   ΣM = 0

3-D Equilibrium (6 equations):
ΣFx = ΣFy = ΣFz = 0   ΣMx = ΣMy = ΣMz = 0

3.2 Free-Body Diagram (FBD)

Support TypeReactions

Weight (W = mg)Downward
Cable/Rope/BeltTension away from body

3.3 Internal Loads in a Beam

  • 3 types of internal loads: Shear force (V), Bending moment (M), Torsional moment (T)
  • Sign conventions:
    • Right-end of cut: +V downward, +M counter-clockwise (beam bends upward)
    • Left-end of cut: +V upward, +M clockwise

3.4 Relations between Load, Shear & Moment

w = −dV/dx  (slope of V = −load)
V = dM/dx  (slope of M = V)
V = ∫w dx  → V = Area under load diagram
M = ∫V dx  → M = Area under shear diagram

V–M Diagrams — Two Methods

Method 1: Static Analysis

  1. Draw FBD
  2. Find all external forces (ΣF=0, ΣM=0)
  3. Virtually cut the beam at each section where loads change
  4. Draw FBD of the cut section with V and M in positive directions
  5. Apply equilibrium to find V and M as functions of x
  6. Draw V–M diagrams
💡 Worked example (simplified):
Beam with point loads: RA = 1 kN, RB = 5 kN
AB section: V = 1 kN, M = x kN·m
BC section: V = −3 kN, M = −3x + 8 kN·m
CD section: V = 2 kN, M = −2x kN·m

Method 2: Singularity Functions

LoadSymbolValue (x < a, x > a)Integration
Concentrated moment ⟨x−a⟩−2 0, 0 ⟨x−a⟩−1
Concentrated force ⟨x−a⟩−1 0, 0 ⟨x−a⟩0
Uniform force distribution ⟨x−a⟩0 0, 1 ⟨x−a⟩1
Ramp force distribution ⟨x−a⟩1 0, (x−a) ⟨x−a⟩2/2
Steps:
1. Draw FBD, find external reactions
ΣM=0, ΣF=0

2. Write load equation w(x) using singularity brackets:
w = RA⟨x−0⟩−1 − F1⟨x−a⟩−1 + F2⟨x−b⟩−1 − RB⟨x−L⟩−1

3. Integrate to get V:  V = ∫w dx

4. Integrate to get M:  M = ∫V dx

5. Evaluate V & M for each section (using the "Value" column)
6. Draw V–M diagrams

Stress Analysis

3.5 Stress Components

  • Normal stress (σ) — perpendicular to the surface plane (tension +, compression −)
  • Shear stress (τ) — parallel to the surface plane
  • In 3-D: 9 components → under equilibrium, cross-shears are equal: τxy=τyx, τxz=τzx, τyz=τzy → 6 independent components
  • Plane stress (2-D): σz = τxz = τyz = 0 → only σx, σy, τxy

3.6 Plane Stress Transformation Equations

Normal stress on a plane at angle φ:
σ = (σx+σy)/2 + (σx−σy)/2 · cos 2φ + τxy · sin 2φ

Shear stress on a plane at angle φ:
τ = −(σx−σy)/2 · sin 2φ + τxy · cos 2φ

Principal Normal Stresses (max & min σ)

Direction:
tan 2φp = 2τxy / (σx − σy)

Magnitude:
σ₁, σ₂ = (σx+σy)/2 ± √[((σx−σy)/2)² + τxy²]
On the principal normal stress planes, τ = 0

Principal Shear Stresses (max & min τ)

Direction:
tan 2φs = −(σx−σy) / (2τxy)

Magnitude:
τ₁, τ₂ = ± √[((σx−σy)/2)² + τxy²]
On the principal shear planes, σ = (σx+σy)/2 (average normal stress)

3.7 Mohr's Circle

  1. Center: C = (σx+σy)/2 on σ-axis
  2. Radius: R = √[((σx−σy)/2)² + τxy²]
  3. Plot point X (σx, τxy) and Y (σy, −τxy)
  4. σ₁ = C + R (rightmost point), σ₂ = C − R (leftmost point)
  5. τmax = R (top of circle)
  6. Angle 2φp measured from point X to σ₁
⭐ EXAMS LOVE: Finding principal stresses at critical point of a shaft under combined bending + torsion:
σ = 32M/(πd³)  (bending)
τ = 16T/(πd³)  (torsion)
σ₁,₂ = σ/2 ± √[(σ/2)² + τ²]
τ_max = √[(σ/2)² + τ²]

3.8 Strain & Poisson's Ratio

Normal strain: ε = δ/l (extension +, compression −)
Shear strain: γ = δ/h (angular)
Poisson's ratio: ν = εlateral / εaxial  (0 ≤ ν ≤ 0.5; metals ≈ 0.3)
Shear modulus: G = E / [2(1+ν)]
Elastic modulus (bulk): K = E / [3(1−2ν)]

Shape Analysis — Second Area Moment of Inertia

Common Shapes

ShapeArea (A)Centroid (y)Moment of Inertia (I)
Rectangle (b×h) bh h/2 from base I = bh³/12 (about centroid)
Circle (diameter d) πd²/4 d/2 I = πd⁴/64
Solid circular shaft πd²/4 d/2 I = πd⁴/64, J = πd⁴/32
Hollow shaft π(dₒ²−dᵢ²)/4 dₒ/2 J = π(dₒ⁴−dᵢ⁴)/32

Parallel Axis Theorem

I = Ic + A·d²
I_c = moment of inertia about centroid axis; d = distance from centroid axis to new axis

Composite Shape — Finding Centroid

ȳ = Σ(Aᵢ·yᵢ) / ΣAᵢ
Divide the shape into simple rectangles, find each area and its centroid, combine.

3.9 Bending Stress in Beams

Normal (bending) stress: σ = −M·c / I
M = bending moment, c = distance from neutral axis, I = second moment of inertia
Max bending stress (solid round shaft): σ = 32M / (πd³)

Shear stress in beams: τ = V·Q / (I·b)
V = shear force, Q = first moment of area above the point = A′·ȳ′, b = width at the point, I = moment of inertia
💡 For a rectangular cross-section: τmax = 1.5·V/A (at neutral axis, parabolic distribution).

Stress Analysis in Mechanical Parts Under Loads

3.10 Torsion of Circular Shafts

Torsional shear stress: τ = T·c / J = 16T / (πd³) (solid shaft)
Polar moment of inertia:
Solid: J = πd⁴/32  |  Hollow: J = π(dₒ⁴ − dᵢ⁴)/32
Note: J = 2I for circular cross-sections

3.11 Combined Loading — Shaft with Torsion + Bending

⚠️ THIS IS A PAST EXAM PROBLEM TYPE — MASTER THE 6-STEP METHOD

The 6-Step Method (memorize this order)

  1. Torque equilibrium → find belt tensions (Torque = Force × Radius)
  2. FBD + equilibrium → find bearing reactions (ΣM=0, ΣF=0)
  3. V–M diagram → find Mmax (critical value)
  4. Bending stress: σ = 32M/(πd³)
  5. Torsional shear: τ = 16T/(πd³)
  6. Principal stresses: σ₁,₂ = σ/2 ± √[(σ/2)² + τ²] and τmax = √[(σ/2)² + τ²]

📝 Worked Example 1: Shaft with two V-belt pulleys (Ch 3)

Tensions at pulley A: T₁ = 500 lbf, T₂ = 75 lbf on 4-in radius. At pulley B, loose side T₂ᵦ = 15% of tight side T₁ᵦ on 5-in radius.

  1. Torque equilibrium: (500×4) − (75×4) − (T₂×5) + (T₁×5) = 0, T₁ = 0.15T₂ → T₂ = 400 lbf, T₁ = 60 lbf
  2. Reactions: ΣM_O = 0 → R_C = 178.25 lbf; R_O = 293.25 lbf
  3. V–M diagram: Mmax = 2932.5 lbf·in
  4. Bending stress: σ = 32M/(πd³)
  5. Torsional shear: τ = 16T/(πd³)
  6. Principal stresses: σ₁,₂ = σ/2 ± √[(σ/2)² + τ²]

📝 Worked Example 2: Full numerical solution (d = 50 mm)

Given: Shaft d = 50 mm, Mmax = 1000 N·m, T = 600 N·m

Step 4 — Bending stress:

σ = 32M/(πd³) = 32(1000×10³)/(π × 50³)
σ = 32,000,000 / 392,699 = 81.5 MPa

Step 5 — Torsional shear:

τ = 16T/(πd³) = 16(600×10³)/(π × 50³)
τ = 9,600,000 / 392,699 = 24.4 MPa

Step 6 — Principal stresses:

σ₁,₂ = σ/2 ± √[(σ/2)² + τ²] = 81.5/2 ± √[(81.5/2)² + 24.4²]
= 40.75 ± √(1660.6 + 595.4) = 40.75 ± √2256 = 40.75 ± 47.5
σ₁ = 88.2 MPa (max normal), σ₂ = −6.75 MPa (min normal)
τmax = 47.5 MPa

📝 Past Exam Problem 3 pattern (from answer key)

Shaft d = 50 mm (r = 25 mm), handle force 200 N at 0.3 m, belt tension T at 0.15 m radius.

  1. Torque: (200)(0.3) = (T)(0.15) → T = 400 N
  2. Reactions: ΣM about A → R_A = 20 N, R_D = 100 N
  3. Singularity functions for w, V, M (with belt tensions F_B, F_C)
  4. Mmax from V–M diagram
  5. τmax = 16T/(πd³) = 16(60)/(π×0.05³) = 11,317.68 Pa
💡 KEY INSIGHT: For a solid round shaft, J = 2I. So τ = Tc/J = 16T/πd³ and σ = Mc/I = 32M/πd³. The factor of 2 difference comes from J = 2I. Always convert M and T to consistent units (N·mm or N·m) before plugging in.

🔄 Chapter 2 Concept Quick-Review Flashcards

  • Stress = Force / original area
  • Strain = Change in length / original length
  • Young's Modulus = slope of elastic region of stress-strain curve
  • Yield point: strain increases rapidly, stress does not
  • Ultimate strength = highest point on stress-strain curve
  • Toughness = area under the entire stress-strain curve
  • Ductility = plastic deformation under TENSION
  • Malleability = plastic deformation under COMPRESSION
  • Hardness = resistance to penetration (Rockwell HR=E−e, Brinell HB)
  • Hot working = above recrystallization temp (strength↑ ductility↑)
  • Cold working = at room temp (yield↑ ultimate↑ hardness↑ ductility↓)
  • Annealing: slow cooling in furnace → ductility ↑
  • Normalising: cooling in air → ductility ↑, finer grain
  • Quenching: rapid cooling in water/oil → hardness ↑, brittleness ↑
  • Tempering: reheat below critical → hardness ↓, brittleness ↓
  • Cast iron: >2.1% C, brittle, cannot weld
  • Steel: <2.1% C, can be hardened & tempered, absorbs shock
  • Wrought iron: very low C, purest form, malleable/ductile
  • Ceramics: metal + non-metal, strong bonds → hard, stiff, not conductive
  • Thermoplastic: can remold when heated, recyclable
  • Thermoset: cannot remold, burns if reheated
  • Composite = Filler + Matrix, direction-dependent properties

    Example A: V–M Diagram with Singularity Functions (Full Solution)

    Problem

    Simply supported beam, span 10 m. Supports at A (x=0) and B (x=10). Point load 4 kN at x=6, distributed load 2 kN/m from x=0 to x=2.

    Step 1: Find reactions

    ΣM_B = 0: R_A(10) − 2(2)(9) − 4(4) = 0
    10R_A − 36 − 16 = 0 → R_A = 5.2 kN
    ΣF_y = 0: R_A + R_B − 4 − 4 = 0 → R_B = 2.8 kN

    Step 2: Write load equation w(x)

    w = 5.2⟨x−0⟩⁻¹ − 2⟨x−0⟩⁰ + 2⟨x−2⟩⁰ − 4⟨x−6⟩⁻¹ − 2.8⟨x−10⟩⁻¹
    Note: distributed load starts at 0, ends at 2 (add +2⟨x−2⟩⁰ to cancel)

    Step 3: Integrate to get V

    V = 5.2⟨x−0⟩⁰ − 2⟨x−0⟩¹ + 2⟨x−2⟩¹ − 4⟨x−6⟩⁰ − 2.8⟨x−10⟩⁰

    Step 4: Integrate to get M

    M = 5.2⟨x−0⟩¹ − ⟨x−0⟩² + ⟨x−2⟩² − 4⟨x−6⟩¹ − 2.8⟨x−10⟩¹

    Step 5: Evaluate sections

    SectionV(x)M(x)
    0 ≤ x < 2V = 5.2 − 2xM = 5.2x − x²
    2 ≤ x < 6V = 5.2 − 2x + 2(x−2) = 1.2M = 5.2x − x² + (x−2)²
    6 ≤ x ≤ 10V = 1.2 − 4 = −2.8M = 5.2x − x² + (x−2)² − 4(x−6)

    Step 6: Find M_max

    M_max occurs where V = 0: 5.2 − 2x = 0 → x = 2.6 m
    M(2.6) = 5.2(2.6) − (2.6)² = 13.52 − 6.76 = 6.76 kN·m

    Example B: Composite Beam — Bending & Shear Stress (Full Solution)

    Problem

    T-shaped beam cross-section: flange 100mm × 20mm on top, web 20mm × 80mm below. M = 10 kN·m, V = 15 kN. Find σ_max and τ_max.

    Step 1: Find centroid (neutral axis)

    A₁ (flange) = 100×20 = 2000 mm², y₁ = 90 mm (from bottom)
    A₂ (web) = 20×80 = 1600 mm², y₂ = 40 mm
    ȳ = (2000×90 + 1600×40) / (2000+1600) = (180000+64000)/3600 = 67.8 mm

    Step 2: Find I about neutral axis (parallel axis theorem)

    I₁ = (100×20³)/12 + 2000(90−67.8)² = 66,667 + 2000(22.2)² = 66,667 + 985,680 = 1,052,347 mm⁴
    I₂ = (20×80³)/12 + 1600(67.8−40)² = 853,333 + 1600(27.8)² = 853,333 + 1,236,544 = 2,089,877 mm⁴
    I_total = 1,052,347 + 2,089,877 = 3,142,224 mm⁴

    Step 3: Bending stress (max at outer fiber)

    c_max = 100 − 67.8 = 32.2 mm (top) or 67.8 mm (bottom)
    σ_max = Mc/I = (10×10⁶)(67.8)/(3,142,224) = 215.8 MPa (at bottom, tension)

    Step 4: Shear stress (max at neutral axis)

    Q at NA = A₁(90−67.8) = 2000(22.2) = 44,400 mm³
    τ_max = VQ/(Ib) = (15×10³)(44,400)/(3,142,224 × 20) = 10.6 MPa

    Example C: Shaft Torsion + Bending (Different Numbers)

    Problem

    Solid shaft d = 40 mm. M_max = 800 N·m, T = 300 N·m. Find σ, τ, principal stresses, τ_max.

    Step 1: Bending stress

    σ = 32M/(πd³) = 32(800×10³)/(π × 40³) = 25,600,000/201,062 = 127.3 MPa

    Step 2: Torsional shear

    τ = 16T/(πd³) = 16(300×10³)/(π × 40³) = 4,800,000/201,062 = 23.9 MPa

    Step 3: Principal stresses

    σ₁,₂ = 127.3/2 ± √[(127.3/2)² + 23.9²] = 63.65 ± √(4051 + 571) = 63.65 ± √4622
    = 63.65 ± 68.0 → σ₁ = 131.6 MPa, σ₂ = −4.3 MPa
    τ_max = 68.0 MPa

    Example D: Interference Fit (Full Solution)

    Problem

    Hole: D_max = 50.02 mm, D_min = 50.00 mm. Shaft (hub): d_max = 60.05 mm, d_min = 60.00 mm. E = 200 GPa. Find P_max.

    Step 1: Find δ_max

    δ_max = (Shaft_max − Hole_min)/2 = (60.05 − 50.00)/2 = 5.025 mm

    Step 2: Convert to radii

    Rᵢ = 50.00/2 = 25 mm (inner/shaft radius)
    Rₒ = 60.00/2 = 30 mm (outer/hub radius)

    Step 3: Apply pressure formula

    P = E·δ_max·(Rₒ²−Rᵢ²)/(2·Rᵢ·Rₒ²)
    = (200×10³)(5.025)(30²−25²)/(2×25×30²)
    = (200×10³)(5.025)(900−625)/(2×25×900)
    = (200×10³)(5.025)(275)/(45,000)
    = 276,375,000/45,000 = 6,141.7 MPa

    Example E: Plane Stress Transformation (Full Solution)

    Problem

    σ_x = 80 MPa, σ_y = 20 MPa, τ_xy = 30 MPa. Find principal stresses and max shear.

    Step 1: Average stress & radius

    σ_avg = (σ_x+σ_y)/2 = (80+20)/2 = 50 MPa
    R = √[((σ_x−σ_y)/2)² + τ_xy²] = √[(30)² + 30²] = √(900+900) = √1800 = 42.4 MPa

    Step 2: Principal stresses

    σ₁ = 50 + 42.4 = 92.4 MPa
    σ₂ = 50 − 42.4 = 7.6 MPa
    τ_max = 42.4 MPa

    Step 3: Principal direction

    tan 2φ_p = 2τ_xy/(σ_x−σ_y) = 2(30)/(80−20) = 60/60 = 1
    2φ_p = 45° → φ_p = 22.5°

    Example F: Brinell Hardness → Strength (Ch 2)

    Problem

    A steel member has H_B = 275. Estimate S_u in MPa. Then a grey cast iron part has H_B = 200. Estimate S_u in kpsi.

    Solution

    Steel: S_u = 3.4 × H_B = 3.4 × 275 = 935 MPa
    Cast iron: S_u = 0.23 × H_B − 12.5 = 0.23(200) − 12.5 = 46 − 12.5 = 33.5 kpsi
    Compare to Table A: ASTM No. 35 grey cast iron
    💡 HOW TO SOLVE ANY PROBLEM: Always write down the formula first, plug in numbers with units, then compute. Check units are consistent (N·mm vs N·m). For shafts, remember J = 2I. For composite beams, always find the centroid FIRST before computing I.

    🧮 V–M Diagram & Singularity Functions — Worked Practice

    💡 THE 3 RULES OF SINGULARITY FUNCTIONS:
    1. Bracket: ⟨x−a⟩ⁿ = 0 when x < a, and = (x−a)ⁿ when x ≥ a
    2. Integration: ∫⟨x−a⟩ⁿ dx = ⟨x−a⟩ⁿ⁺¹/(n+1)
    3. Chain: w → V → M (integrate twice, always)

    📝 PRACTICE PROBLEM A: Simply Supported Beam (Full Solution)

    Problem: Span 10 m, support A at x=0, support B at x=10. Point load 4 kN at x=6, distributed load 2 kN/m from x=0 to x=2.

    Step 1 — Find reactions:

    ΣM_B = 0: R_A(10) − 2(2)(9) − 4(4) = 0 → 10R_A − 36 − 16 = 0 → R_A = 5.2 kN
    ΣF_y = 0: R_A + R_B − 4 − 4 = 0 → R_B = 2.8 kN
    Distributed load total = 2 kN/m × 2 m = 4 kN acting at midpoint (9 m from B)

    Step 2 — Write load equation w(x):

    w = 5.2⟨x−0⟩⁻¹ − 2⟨x−0⟩⁰ + 2⟨x−2⟩⁰ − 4⟨x−6⟩⁻¹ − 2.8⟨x−10⟩⁻¹

    ⚠️ The +2⟨x−2⟩⁰ CANCELS the distributed load after x=2! Without it, the load goes to infinity.

    Step 3 — Integrate w → V:

    V = 5.2⟨x−0⟩⁰ − 2⟨x−0⟩¹ + 2⟨x−2⟩¹ − 4⟨x−6⟩⁰ − 2.8⟨x−10⟩⁰

    Step 4 — Integrate V → M:

    M = 5.2⟨x−0⟩¹ − ⟨x−0⟩² + ⟨x−2⟩² − 4⟨x−6⟩¹ − 2.8⟨x−10⟩¹

    Step 5 — Evaluate each section (drop inactive brackets):

    SectionV(x)M(x)
    0 ≤ x < 2V = 5.2 − 2xM = 5.2x − x²
    2 ≤ x < 6V = 5.2 − 2x + 2(x−2) = 1.2M = 5.2x − x² + (x−2)²
    6 ≤ x ≤ 10V = 1.2 − 4 = −2.8M = 5.2x − x² + (x−2)² − 4(x−6)

    Step 6 — Find M_max (V = 0):

    M_max occurs where V = 0 → 5.2 − 2x = 0 → x = 2.6 m
    M(2.6) = 5.2(2.6) − (2.6)² = 13.52 − 6.76 = 6.76 kN·m

    Check: V(10) = −2.8 = −R_B ✓, M(10) = 0 ✓ (simply supported ends)

    📝 PRACTICE PROBLEM B: Cantilever Beam (Full Solution)

    Problem: Cantilever length 6 m, uniform load w₀ = 3 kN/m over entire length, fixed at A.

    Step 1 — Reactions at fixed support A:

    R_A = w₀L = 3(6) = 18 kN ↑
    M_A = w₀L²/2 = 3(36)/2 = 54 kN·m (CW)

    Step 2 — w(x): Only the distributed load from A:

    w = −3⟨x−0⟩⁰

    Step 3 — V(x) = ∫w dx:

    V = −3⟨x−0⟩¹ = −3x   (from 0 to 6)
    At x=6: V = −18 kN ✓ (matches R_A)

    Step 4 — M(x) = ∫V dx:

    M = −(3/2)⟨x−0⟩² = −1.5x²
    At x=6: M = −1.5(36) = −54 kN·m ✓ (matches M_A)

    Step 5 — Draw: V is linear 0 → −18 kN; M is parabolic 0 → −54 kN·m.

    HOW TO READ A V–M DIAGRAM (exam shortcuts):
    • V jumps up → upward point load/reaction at that x
    • V jumps down → downward point load at that x
    • V is constant → no distributed load in that section
    • V is sloped line → uniform load w = −slope
    • V crosses zero → M is MAX here — the critical value you use for stress!
    • M is linear → V is constant there
    • M is parabolic → V is linear there (under uniform load)
